# House voice

How the course writes, in chapters, exercises, glosses and slides. The level and
audience are in [Editorial direction](../project/editorial-direction.md); this
note is about sentences.

We write to an AI undergraduate who is reading before class. "We" is the course
making a move — "In this chapter, we'll study formal languages"; "you" is the
reader doing something — "you'll learn how". Say the thing, then say what it
means. A definition is stated; an explanation follows it; an example follows
that.

## Do not write

- **Significance announcements.** "This matters because …", "It is important to
  note that …", "Notice that this is crucial." If it matters, the sentence that
  shows why is the content; the announcement replaces it with a claim.
- **Contrastive framing as explanation.** "It's a function, not a formula",
  "this isn't about X, it's about Y." Define what the thing is. Raise a genuine
  confusion only where a reader will actually have it, and then as its own
  sentence in the running text, never inside a callout.
- **Throat-clearing.** "Let's dive in", "As we saw above, and as we'll see
  again", "In today's world", restating the section title as the first sentence.
- **Filler emphasis.** "very", "really", "simply", "just", "of course" where it
  smuggles in that a step is easy. A reader who finds it hard reads that as a
  verdict on themselves.
- **Summaries of what is about to happen** beyond the one bridging sentence that
  carries the chapter's through-line forward.

## In callouts

A `definition` or `theorem` callout carries the statement alone: no motivation,
no example, no contrast, no significance. Everything else goes in the paragraphs
around it. See [Chapter standards](chapter-standards.md) and
[Callouts](callouts.md).
